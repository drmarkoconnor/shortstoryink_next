## The ethos to retain

The governing promise is: “Write, receive precise feedback in place, reflect, revise, and grow within a lasting literary community.” The full loop is Read → Notice → Save → Write → Submit → Receive feedback → Reflect → Revise → Re-enter community.

Its audience is adult beginners and intermediate writers, initially the existing physical group. It should help people feel seen, encouraged, challenged, guided and less alone. The teacher’s taste and generosity matter alongside standards. Writing psychology belongs in the teaching: reducing inhibition, finding momentum and continuing after difficulty.

Five commitments should govern the course:

1. Teach through passages and particular choices, with comments beside the words they concern.
2. Help writers develop their own judgment and voice. Exercises offer choices and experiments; they are not universal prescriptions.
3. Treat reading and the commonplace book as learning activities in their own right.
4. Keep community personal, teacher-led and private by default. Sharing is deliberate.
5. Make the structure clear enough to support people without turning participation into completion scores or administrative work.

The approved move to a light paper-like palette does not betray the founding aesthetic. Dark-first was an early implementation preference; calm, legibility, space and literary character are the enduring aims. Likewise the documented framework and hosting changes supersede the old stack instructions. Those historical details should not drive a reversal of the current product.

## Proposed course: The Short Story Workshop — from noticing to revision

A flexible twelve-session sequence, with timing to be agreed. Sessions can span more than one meeting. Returning writers can work on an existing piece; beginners can build one from an observation. Everyone can return to the reading shelf and restart material at any point.

By the end, a writer should be able to notice and explain a craft choice in a passage; gather attributed examples and personal observations; draft a story or substantial scene; make deliberate choices about person, place, viewpoint, time and structure; and revise in response to feedback while retaining their own intention.

| Session | Teaching focus | Practice and existing starting material |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Begin again | Noticing, notebook/commonplace, freewriting and permission to experiment | Five minutes of rough writing; choose one live detail; try a tiny complete story. Use restart notes. |
| 2. Someone particular | Character, desire, contradiction, agency and pressure | Reveal someone through a small action. Use What is a Story? and the stuckness guide. |
| 3. Somewhere that matters | Location, sensory selection, atmosphere, setting as pressure | Write the same arrival in two places. Use scene illustrations and Groundhog Day. |
| 4. Who tells it? | First/third person, knowledge, distance, voice and bias | Retell a moment from two positions and explain the effect. Develop the existing viewpoint guidance. |
| 5. Make a scene | Action, exposition, showing/telling and scene versus summary | Expand one summary into a scene, then decide which version the story needs. Rework Scene Checker. |
| 6. What remains unsaid | Dialogue, subtext, idiolect, silence and action beats | Two people discuss an ordinary object while wanting different things. Use a short annotated example. |
| 7. What happens because of this? | Openings, reader expectations, causality, stakes and alternative structures | Try three openings; trace choices and consequences, allowing discovery and quiet narrative forms. Use Plot and Openings. |
| 8. The story’s clock | Duration, sequence, backstory, compression, expansion and pacing | Compress years; slow a hinge moment; apply one choice to the developing story. Use the time cluster. |
| 9. Images that carry meaning | Concrete detail, metaphor, motif and restraint | Revisit an object so its meaning changes. Use selected rhetoric material and Stone Cold Memories. |
| 10. Where to leave the reader | Endings, resonance, theme and the beginning’s promise | Write two endings; say how each changes the meaning. Develop Openings and the circular example. |
| 11. Make it clear; keep it alive | Sentence/paragraph rhythm, precision, transitions and line editing | Read aloud and revise a paragraph for clarity without flattening its voice. Curate rhetoric examples. |
| 12. Return to the story | Feedback, structural revision before polishing, reflection and continuing practice | Revise an earlier draft; describe one choice changed, one retained and the next experiment. Use feedback packets. |

This is a sequence of emphases, not a ban on revising or discussing endings earlier. Read, write and revise from session one. Revisit the same short passages with different questions so techniques accumulate through use. Select readings across voices, settings and narrative approaches; the existing illustrations should not determine the cultural range of the writing.

Every session needs the same modest package: a short invitation, one outcome, an attributed passage, two noticing questions, one manageable exercise, an optional stretch, one revision prompt and a next step. Aim for a concise printable handout with extension material separately linked. Define a session as ready only when these pieces actually connect.

Keep formal feedback manageable: provisionally one scene after session 4, a story draft after session 8 and a revision after session 12. Other exercises can remain private or be discussed in class. Confirm group size and Mark’s available review time before promising turnaround or word limits. Offer a small core task and optional extension; absence should lead to an easy return, not a backlog of compulsory exercises.

## Recommended next focus and return to students

First, make one coherent entrance into the course. A short “Start here / This session / Reading and practice / What next” page can link the existing group-visible handouts and examples. Label a current session explicitly; a recently updated document is not necessarily the next lesson. Keep the library available for browsing. An ordered handout can pilot this immediately; software can then add a lightweight ordered group course page. Preserve existing access checks.

Before invitations, rehearse one returning writer’s access to the intended group and material, submission, published feedback and revision. The migration handoff says existing confirmed users need a one-time Forgot password reset; verify that email journey rather than assuming previous automated tests covered mail delivery. Confirm what working drafts recover locally versus what has been submitted to the service, and give students a clear contact route if they get stuck.

Prepare the course overview and first two complete session packs, plus a brief “How we read each other’s work” note. Frame feedback as specific observation, effect and possibility; the writer remains the author. Keep student work out of shared course examples unless the relevant permission has been obtained. This follows the existing privacy and export principles.

Next, add a minimal private writer commonplace: selected passage, source, why it matters, optional craft tag, retrieval and a link back to the reading. Do not add a social wall at the same time. Until it exists, invite a paper or personal digital notebook so the pedagogical habit begins immediately.

Then strengthen the feedback-to-revision bridge: “What am I trying to change?”, a chosen reading/exercise, and a short reflection after revising. Keep this light and useful; do not introduce grading rubrics or mandatory logs simply to make the programme look like a course.

Review the first returning cohort through observable questions: Can someone identify what to do next? Find the right passage? Make a deliberate craft choice? Understand feedback? Produce a meaningful revision? Return after missing a meeting? Can Mark sustain the review load? These are better early measures than pages viewed or modules completed.

The recommended priority order is therefore: returning-student access rehearsal; course map and first session packs; a small writer commonplace; reflection/revision continuity; then wider content completion. Further visual expansion, open peer discussion, billing, certificates and complex course machinery should wait.
